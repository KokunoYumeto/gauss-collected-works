Volume V covers physical leaves 2–122, 201–291, 351–542, the Schumacher letter beginning within leaf 546 through leaf 574 before the Erläuterungen, and 586–648. Its historical contents also name writings outside this selection. The Allgemeine Theorie des Erdmagnetismus and attached maps, the intervening unavailable work, the Copenhagen/Mailand graphical report, and the later Erläuterungen with charts are outside this reader.

Volume VI follows the supplied collected edition. It does not supply original periodical passages, ephemerides, maps, catalogues or the Iris calculation absent from that printing. Volume VII contains the complete first book and the opening of the second book through article 130 in the upper part of native leaf 178. Its 173 pages comprise 172 pages of historical content and one modern authorship and source notice. It includes aberration, light-travel time, inverse coordinate conversion and differential relations for geocentric position, with the complete numerical example and its formulation through the orbital elements. The third section presents trigonometric relations, the determination of a conic orbit from two radii and a given semiparameter, perihelion direction or eccentricity, and the determination from three radii. It then treats the problem of determining an orbit from two radii and the elapsed time, including an iterative method and the complete approximation derivation, formulas I–VIII and the historical Euler citations, followed by two complete numerical examples comparing the precision of those approximations, and the elliptical relations between two orbital places, with formulas 1–12 and four starred counterparts, followed by an auxiliary-angle transformation and the formulas for l and L, the series and continued-fraction expansions of the auxiliary expression, the coefficient laws, both historical notes and the range of the third auxiliary table. The cubic equations, successive approximation and choice of the applicable root follow, with the historical notes and the construction and interpolation of the second auxiliary table. The trial solution for larger auxiliary angles and the possible multiplicity when complete revolutions intervene are followed by the determination of orbital elements and anomalies from the auxiliary angle, with formulas for the semiaxes, parameter, eccentricity and epoch. Further half-angle formulas give the orbital elements with their ambiguity conditions and calculation checks. Three complete numerical examples determine the orbital elements and anomalies, followed by the perihelion-time calculation and its precision qualifications. The parabolic case includes its complete derivation and the determination of the elements. The hyperbolic case includes the relations between two orbital places, the auxiliary quantities, their series and continued-fraction expansions, the equations used to determine the hyperbolic arc, the recovery of orbital elements and perihelion time, a complete numerical example for a hyperbolic orbit, and the elapsed-time formula in terms of the semimajor axis, sum of radii and chord, with its historical attribution and ambiguity discussion. The following articles present the elapsed-time series and the independent parabolic derivation, with both historical manuscript notes on chord geometry, time and perihelion distance. The third section concludes with the hyperbolic elapsed-time derivation and both series. The fourth section opens with the determination of the orbital plane from two heliocentric places using longitude and latitude or rectangular coordinates, including ambiguity conditions, projected areas and the choice of reference plane. Article 112 relates three heliocentric positions through projected triangle areas and gives the complete equations in geocentric and heliocentric quantities. Article 113 introduces the abbreviated three-place expressions, their signed permutations and complete list, and their interpretation through the volume of a pyramid. Article 114 gives the elimination formulas for three geocentric distances and their exceptional cases, concluding the fourth section and first book. The second book opens with the data required to determine an orbit from geocentric observations and the exceptional ecliptic-plane case. Articles 116–117 give the reduction of observations to a fixed reference plane and three treatments of parallax. Articles 118–122 give the treatments of aberration, the reduction to two implicit equations, and the approximation and iteration procedures for determining their unknowns, with the historical note on the order of the omitted terms. Articles 123–128 treat the choice and spacing of observations and five methods using two assumed distances. They compare geocentric positions, heliocentric positions, elapsed times, pairs of orbital elements or quantities calculated before the elements themselves, retaining their nodal, small-arc and near-parabolic limitations and precision considerations. The complete fifth method includes its logarithmic comparison and final choice of observations for determining the elements. Article 129 gives five corresponding methods using inclination and ascending-node longitude, with their nodal restrictions. Article 130 sets out the three requirements for an initial orbit-determination method and introduces the preliminary considerations. Its source is the 1906 collected printing of the 1809 Theoria motus.
