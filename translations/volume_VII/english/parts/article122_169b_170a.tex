The values assigned to the unknowns in the second and third hypotheses are to some extent arbitrary, provided they do not differ too much from those in the first hypothesis and we take care that the ratio $(a''-a):(b''-b)$ does not approach equality with $(a'-a):(b'-b)$. We therefore usually set $a'=a$, $b''=b$. This has two advantages. The formulas for $\xi,\eta$ become a little simpler still, and one part of the first calculation remains the same in the second hypothesis, while another part remains the same in the third.

There is, however, a case in which other considerations favor departing from this practice. Suppose that $X$ has the form $X'-x$ and that $Y$ has the form $Y'-y$, and that the nature of the problem is such that the functions $X',Y'$ are very little affected by moderate errors in the values of $x,y$. That is, $\left(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}X'}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)\mathpunct{\raisebox{.45ex}{,}}$ $\left(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}X'}{\mathrm{d}y}\right)\mathpunct{\raisebox{.45ex}{,}}$ $\left(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Y'}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)\mathpunct{\raisebox{.45ex}{,}}$ and $\left(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Y'}{\mathrm{d}y}\right)$ are very small quantities. It is then clear that the differences \SourceLeaf{170}between the values of those functions corresponding to $x=\xi$, $y=\eta$ and the values obtained from $x=a$, $y=b$ may be regarded as being, so to speak, of a higher order than the differences $\xi-a$, $\eta-b$. The former values are $X'=\xi$, $Y'=\eta$, and the latter are $X'=a+A$, $Y'=b+B$. It follows that $a+A$, $b+B$ are much more accurate values of $x,y$ than $a,b$. If we base the second hypothesis on these values, the equations $X=0$, $Y=0$ are very often already satisfied so accurately that there is no need to go any further. Otherwise we form the third hypothesis from the second in the same way by setting $a''=a'+A'=a+A+A'$, $b''=b'+B'=b+B+B'$. If this still proves insufficiently precise, we finally obtain a fourth hypothesis in accordance with Article 120.
