# Carl Friedrich Gauss: Werke, Band VII — Opening, Articles 1–192, Tables I–III, Figures 1–4, Bemerkungen and Zusätze 1–18 with their editorial remarks; Parabolic Motion, Notes 1–9

This original-language partial edition contains the four historical title leaves, the complete Latin Praefatio, the Contenta and the first one hundred and ninety-two articles of *Theoria motus corporum coelestium*. These articles present the opening laws, the calculation of the constant k, the gravitational setting, the geometrical relations for ellipses, parabolas and hyperbolas, the relations between orbital position, anomaly and elapsed time, the geometrical interpretation of eccentric anomaly and the minor semiaxis, a numerical example calculating eccentric and mean anomaly from true anomaly, and an iterative solution of Kepler’s equation with guidance on choosing an initial approximation and a complete numerical example, followed by three methods for calculating true anomaly and radius vector from eccentric anomaly, the differential relations between anomalies and radius vector, and the maxima of the equation of centre with a numerical comparison for Juno, the relation between true anomaly and elapsed time in a parabolic orbit, the use of Barker’s table, differential relations for parabolic motion, auxiliary quantities and formulas for hyperbolic motion, and numerical examples for calculating elapsed time in a hyperbolic orbit with common and hyperbolic logarithms, followed by the inverse problem and a numerical example determining true anomaly and radius vector from elapsed time, differential relations for hyperbolic motion, and relations between the auxiliary imaginary quantities and the real quantities used for hyperbolas, and the precision of logarithmic tables, interpolation and propagation of errors in arithmetic operations, with detailed error bounds for the elliptic and hyperbolic calculations and two numerical tables, the limitations of those methods near the parabolic case, and series relating the true anomalies in elliptic or hyperbolic motion to the corresponding parabolic anomaly, the use and limitations of tables for those series, and error bounds at larger values of the auxiliary angles, with two further numerical tables, followed by a formulation using two auxiliary quantities and a table of logarithmic values and a numerical example calculating elapsed time in an eccentric ellipse, and an approximation method using Barker’s table for the inverse problem, formulas using auxiliary quantities for true anomaly and radius vector, the inverse calculation of elapsed time, and an account of the auxiliary table’s construction and range, a numerical example calculating true anomaly and radius vector from elapsed time, and auxiliary formulas for hyperbolic motion, with the table and procedures for calculating time from true anomaly and true anomaly from time, illustrated by two complete numerical examples. The second section opens with the representation of orbital planes on a sphere, ascending and descending nodes, inclination, and the definitions of perihelion, true and mean longitude, the seven orbital elements, direct and retrograde motion conventions, and the relations between heliocentric longitude, latitude, argument of latitude and reduced distance, a numerical example for Juno, and differential relations for latitude, longitude and reduced distance, followed by rectangular coordinates, the relation between orbital planes and a general set of spherical triangle formulas, with the conversion to another reference plane, a numerical example for equatorial coordinates, and differential formulas for slowly varying reference planes, followed by direct expressions for distances from given planes using eccentric anomaly in an ellipse or auxiliary quantities in a hyperbola, with a numerical example and the parabolic case, followed by coordinates for Earth’s centre and points on its surface, solar latitude and the determination of geocentric position and distance, the choice of methods with nutation and parallax, and three methods for converting heliocentric coordinates to geocentric coordinates, with numerical examples, the associated triangles and sine relations, differential formulas, inverse coordinate conversion, and two methods for deriving right ascension and declination from ecliptic longitude and latitude, their inverse formulas and differential relations, numerical examples, the determination of parallax for positions on the surface of the Earth, aberration and the travel time of light, a combined numerical example, the inverse determination of heliocentric position, and differential formulas for geocentric longitude and latitude using orbital quantities and auxiliary angles, followed by the complete numerical calculation of their differential coefficients and their expression through the orbital elements.
