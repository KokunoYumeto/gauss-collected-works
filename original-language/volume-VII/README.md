# Carl Friedrich Gauss: Werke, Band VII — Opening and Articles 1–128

This original-language partial edition contains the four historical title leaves, the complete Latin Praefatio, the Contenta and the first one hundred and twenty-eight articles of *Theoria motus corporum coelestium*. These articles present the opening laws, the calculation of the constant k, the gravitational setting, the geometrical relations for ellipses, parabolas and hyperbolas, the relations between orbital position, anomaly and elapsed time, the geometrical interpretation of eccentric anomaly and the minor semiaxis, a numerical example calculating eccentric and mean anomaly from true anomaly, and an iterative solution of Kepler’s equation with guidance on choosing an initial approximation and a complete numerical example, followed by three methods for calculating true anomaly and radius vector from eccentric anomaly, the differential relations between anomalies and radius vector, and the maxima of the equation of centre with a numerical comparison for Juno, the relation between true anomaly and elapsed time in a parabolic orbit, the use of Barker’s table, differential relations for parabolic motion, auxiliary quantities and formulas for hyperbolic motion, and numerical examples for calculating elapsed time in a hyperbolic orbit with common and hyperbolic logarithms, followed by the inverse problem and a numerical example determining true anomaly and radius vector from elapsed time, differential relations for hyperbolic motion, and relations between the auxiliary imaginary quantities and the real quantities used for hyperbolas, and the precision of logarithmic tables, interpolation and propagation of errors in arithmetic operations, with detailed error bounds for the elliptic and hyperbolic calculations and two numerical tables, the limitations of those methods near the parabolic case, and series relating the true anomalies in elliptic or hyperbolic motion to the corresponding parabolic anomaly, the use and limitations of tables for those series, and error bounds at larger values of the auxiliary angles, with two further numerical tables, followed by a formulation using two auxiliary quantities and a table of logarithmic values and a numerical example calculating elapsed time in an eccentric ellipse, and an approximation method using Barker’s table for the inverse problem, formulas using auxiliary quantities for true anomaly and radius vector, the inverse calculation of elapsed time, and an account of the auxiliary table’s construction and range, a numerical example calculating true anomaly and radius vector from elapsed time, and auxiliary formulas for hyperbolic motion, with the table and procedures for calculating time from true anomaly and true anomaly from time, illustrated by two complete numerical examples. The second section opens with the representation of orbital planes on a sphere, ascending and descending nodes, inclination, and the definitions of perihelion, true and mean longitude, the seven orbital elements, direct and retrograde motion conventions, and the relations between heliocentric longitude, latitude, argument of latitude and reduced distance, a numerical example for Juno, and differential relations for latitude, longitude and reduced distance, followed by rectangular coordinates, the relation between orbital planes and a general set of spherical triangle formulas, with the conversion to another reference plane, a numerical example for equatorial coordinates, and differential formulas for slowly varying reference planes, followed by direct expressions for distances from given planes using eccentric anomaly in an ellipse or auxiliary quantities in a hyperbola, with a numerical example and the parabolic case, followed by coordinates for Earth’s centre and points on its surface, solar latitude and the determination of geocentric position and distance, the choice of methods with nutation and parallax, and three methods for converting heliocentric coordinates to geocentric coordinates, with numerical examples, the associated triangles and sine relations, differential formulas, inverse coordinate conversion, and two methods for deriving right ascension and declination from ecliptic longitude and latitude, their inverse formulas and differential relations, numerical examples, the determination of parallax for positions on the surface of the Earth, aberration and the travel time of light, a combined numerical example, the inverse determination of heliocentric position, and differential formulas for geocentric longitude and latitude using orbital quantities and auxiliary angles, followed by the complete numerical calculation of their differential coefficients and their expression through the orbital elements. The preface occupies printed pages 3–10; articles 1–46 comprise the complete first section, and articles 47–77 comprise the complete second section. The third section begins with trigonometric relations and formulas for determining a conic orbit from two radii and a given semiparameter or perihelion direction. The determination from three radii and the introduction to the elapsed-time problem follow. The elapsed-time problem includes the complete approximation derivation and formulas I–VIII, with the historical Euler citations. Two complete numerical examples illustrate the approximation formulas and their precision. The elliptical relations between two orbital places include the complete formulas 1–12 and their four starred counterparts. The auxiliary-angle transformation and both formulas for l and L follow in article 89, which ends on printed page 115. Article 90 gives the series and continued-fraction expansions of the auxiliary expression, the coefficient laws, both historical notes and the range of the third auxiliary table, ending on printed page 118. Articles 91–93 give the cubic equations and their successive approximation, the choice of the applicable root with historical notes, and the construction and interpolation of the second auxiliary table, ending on printed page 121. Article 94 treats the trial solution for larger auxiliary angles and the possible multiplicity when complete revolutions intervene, ending on printed page 122. Article 95 gives the determination of the orbital elements from the auxiliary angle, ending on printed page 124. Article 96 derives further half-angle formulas for the orbital elements, with the ambiguity conditions and calculation checks, ending on printed page 126. Article 97 gives three complete numerical examples, with the determination of the orbital elements, anomalies and perihelion time, ending on printed page 129. Article 98 gives the complete parabolic derivation and determination of the elements, ending on printed page 131. Articles 99–100 give the hyperbolic relations, the auxiliary quantities and the series and continued-fraction expansions, ending on printed page 135. Articles 101–103 continue the auxiliary equations and their solution for hyperbolic orbits, ending on printed page 137. Article 104 derives the orbital elements and perihelion time for hyperbolic motion, ending on printed page 139. Article 105 gives a complete numerical calculation for a hyperbolic orbit. Article 106 derives the elapsed-time formula from the semimajor axis, sum of radii and chord, with its historical attribution and ambiguity discussion, ending on printed page 142. Article 107 gives the two series for elapsed time and their sign conditions. Article 108 derives the parabolic elapsed-time formula independently and includes both historical manuscript notes on the chord geometry, time and perihelion distance. Its body ends in the upper portion of printed page 145, with its second historical note at the foot of that page. Article 109 gives the hyperbolic elapsed-time derivation, both complete series and their relation to the elliptical case, concluding the third section on printed page 147. The fourth section opens with the determination of the orbital plane from two heliocentric places, first using longitude and latitude and then rectangular coordinates. Articles 110–111 retain the ambiguity conditions, projected-area relations and the arbitrary-reference-plane qualification, ending on printed page 150. Article 112 relates three heliocentric positions through their projected triangle areas and gives the complete three equations in geocentric and heliocentric quantities, ending on printed page 151. Article 113 introduces the abbreviated three-place expressions, their signed permutations and complete list, and their interpretation through the volume of a pyramid. It ends in the upper portion of printed page 153. Article 114 gives the elimination formulas for the three geocentric distances and their exceptional cases, concluding the fourth section and the complete first book on printed page 154. The second book begins with orbit determination from geocentric observations. Articles 115–128 give the data requirements, the exceptional ecliptic-plane case, observational reductions to a fixed reference plane, three treatments of parallax and aberration, reduction to two implicit equations, approximation and iteration procedures, the choice and spacing of observations, and five methods using two assumed distances. These methods compare geocentric positions, heliocentric positions, elapsed times, pairs of orbital elements or quantities calculated before the elements themselves. They retain their nodal, small-arc and near-parabolic limitations and precision considerations. Article 128 gives the complete fifth method through its final choice of observations for determining the elements, ending on printed page 169. Article 129, the remainder of the second book, its tables and the rest of Volume VII are outside this edition.
