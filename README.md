## English and German translations

The current translations contain complete Volume I and the Volume II texts through its historical contents and imprint. Volume III contains the historical title matter and the complete 1799 dissertation, Articles 1–24, all ten historical footnotes, the closing October 1797 statement and all four figures in editable LaTeX. Gauss’s discussion of earlier proofs, geometric proof and concluding alternative argument retain their formulations and qualifications. Its originally German title matter is rendered in modern German in the German reader, with the historical German and Latin source bodies and editable figures supplied separately. The complete second proof of 1815 follows, with its title, Articles 1–20 and both historical footnotes. The complete third proof of 1816 follows with its title, introduction, Articles 1–4 and historical footnote. The complete 1828 Proof of an Algebraic Theorem follows, on positive and negative roots and coefficient signs, including missing terms. The complete 1849 Contributions to the Theory of Algebraic Equations follows with its title, introduction, Articles 1–20, both authorial notes and editable plate. The complete reviews of 23 December 1815, 2 March 1816 and 30 July 1849 follow, with the handwritten remarks accompanying the review of 1816, followed by the complete reviews of Coytier of 6 March 1813 and Fourier of 25 February 1833. The complete available First Part (Pars prior) of General Investigations of the Infinite Series follows. Presented on 30 January 1812 and published at Göttingen in 1813, it contains the full series title, Introduction (Articles 1–6), First Section (7–11), Second Section (12–14), Third Section (15–37), both authorial notes and the complete editable table of 102 printed data rows. It treats contiguous functions, continued fractions, sums at unity, the functions Pi and Psi, infinite products, logarithmic series and definite integrals. The First Part retains the introduction’s promise of a fourth section; that promised section is not supplied in this available First Part. The complete A New Method for Finding the Values of Integrals by Approximation (Methodus nova integralium valores per approximationem inveniendi), presented on 16 September 1814 and published at Göttingen in 1816, follows with its title, Articles 1–23, both authorial footnotes, all seven editable coefficient tables and all seven numerical examples for the integral of dx/log x from 100000 to 200000. It treats equally and unequally spaced interpolation values, integral coefficients, correction series, polynomial remainders, continued fractions and the choice of evaluation points. Historical formulas, numerical values and qualifications remain those of Gauss’s source. Newton and Cotes retain their historical roles in Gauss’s account. Gauss cites Bessel’s calculation of the closing integral. The complete reviews of 10 February 1812 and 26 September 1814 follow. They discuss the First Part of the General Investigations of the Infinite Series and A New Method for Finding the Values of Integrals by Approximation. The first includes the continued fractions, Pi and Psi functions, Kramp discussion and Nicolai table account; the second includes the Newton and Cotes history, interpolation and quadrature formulas, coefficient construction, accuracy qualifications and numerical example. The reporters’ third-person accounts of Gauss and their first-person plural remain distinct. Both historical German originals are retained; the German reader gives modern German renderings. The complete posthumous Determination of Our Series by a Differential Equation of the Second Order follows with its heading, Articles 38–57 and Schering’s historical editorial remarks. It retains the differential equations, transformations, continuation discussion, Pi-function formulas and historical numerical values. Schering’s remarks retain the manuscript account, the personal-copy annotations and the remarks on Article 48. The original Latin and German texts are retained separately; the German reader gives a modern German rendering of Schering’s German remarks. The complete review of 1 July 1815 of Pfaff’s Methodus generalis follows. It includes the reductions of differential expressions, the three integration problems, the distinction between the two kinds of partial derivatives, the derivation of transformation I and the concluding exceptional case. The German original is retained separately; the German reader gives a modern German rendering. Historical formulations, including their printed irregularities, remain identifiable. The complete available division Remarks on logarithm tables follows in Volume III. It contains the errata notice of 1802; the reviews of Prasse of 1811 and 1814; the explanatory text of 1812 on logarithms of sums and differences, with all three examples; the reviews of Pasquich (1817), Matthiessen (1819), Babbage (1828) and Hassler (1831); Gauss’s letter to Schumacher of 25 November 1822; the application to quadratic equations of 1840, with its editable tables and example; and the complete remarks of 1851 on Vega’s Thesaurus Logarithmorum, with Gauss’s footnote. The explanatory text of 1812 describes a table; this edition includes its available explanation and examples. German historical originals are retained separately, and the German reader gives modern German renderings. The historical formulations, numerical values, claims and qualifications remain those of the sources. The complete available posthumous Theory of Interpolation Treated by a New Method follows in Volume III, with its title, Articles 1–41, all three historical footnotes and Ernst Schering’s subsequent editorial remarks. It includes the finite-sum identities, trigonometric interpolation and period subdivisions, the Pallas and Juno examples with all seven editable numerical tables, and Schering’s difference and summation formulas with their editable table. The historical Latin and German source files are retained separately. Schering’s originally German remarks are translated into English and rendered in modern German in the German reader. The historical formulations, numerical values, claims and qualifications remain those of the sources. The complete work of 1818 on the attraction of a planetary orbit follows in Volume III with its formal and repeated titles, Articles 1–19 and both historical footnotes. It treats the attraction of an elliptic ring, the algebraic reduction and the arithmetic-geometric algorithm for the resulting integrals. The complete review of 9 February 1818 follows, including its account of the algorithm, the circumference of an ellipse and the duration of pendulum oscillations. The Latin paper and the German review retain their respective historical formulations, conditions, values, claims and qualifications. The original Latin and German source files are retained separately; the review is translated into English and rendered in modern German in the German reader. Volume II retains its selection designation; a complete-volume assembly claim is not made. The rest of the requested translation corpus remains unfinished.

## Authorship and sources

Carl Friedrich Gauss is the author of the historical mathematical text. Historical editors, correspondents and contributors retain their named credits. Original-language text is AI-transcribed, with AI-produced source comparisons and typesetting; the English and German texts are AI-translated. Exact model credits and source versions remain in the accompanying editions and guides. No human or independent review is claimed.

The translation guide credits H. Maser; James Caddall Morehead and Adam Miller Hiltebeitel; Paul Taylor and Bernard Leak; and the other named terminology references for their stated roles. See the [full scholarly credits](docs/english-german.md#translation-sources-and-terminology) and [citation metadata](translations/CITATIONS.bib). AI work does not replace historical or inherited human authorship.

These editions are freely available for study and teaching.
